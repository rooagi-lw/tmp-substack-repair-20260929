Muse reached nearly 9 million downloads in five weeks and Wonder put Meta back at the model frontier. Now the agent has to help pay a $130–145 billion capex bill.

Five weeks ago, Meta stopped being the company that gives AI away and became the company selling an agent. Muse launched on September 8, and the stock has risen about 20% since, according to Reuters — a sharp reversal for a name that lost roughly 10% after second-quarter earnings, when investors looked at the capex guide and sold first. What changed is not the spending. It is that Wall Street now has a revenue story to set against it: Citi models more than $27 billion of Muse revenue by 2030 and calls personal agents "the new front door to the internet." Yesterday, Meta added the missing piece — Muse Wonder, a frontier model that will soon power the agent. The question for META holders is whether any of this converts into money before the depreciation does.

An agent with its own computer

Muse is not a chatbot with tools bolted on. You state a goal — book the trip, lower the bill, sell the car, get the refund — and Muse builds a plan and keeps working after you close the app, returning when something changes or when it needs your approval. It launched in the US for adults on iOS, Android, the web and inside WhatsApp, with a Mac app following in mid-September and a Muse for Small Business tier on September 29.

The architecture is the pitch. Every user gets a dedicated cloud computer, the Muse Secure VM, and a separate system called Sentinel must approve every connector action and every byte of internet traffic. Credentials live outside the agent's workspace, so Muse can use a login or a payment card without ever seeing the password or the number. Purchases run through single-use cards from Link by Stripe, and sensitive actions — sending email, spending money — pause for approval.

Pricing is usage, not features. The free tier reportedly allows up to 100 million Muse tokens a week; Power costs $20 a month for 500 million; Maximum costs $100 for 3 billion. Paid plans buy more usage, not extra capabilities. Fewer than 200 people built the product, and Reuters reported Meta delayed the launch after internal testing raised concerns about how the agent handled sensitive information — worth remembering when we get to the risks.

Adoption: real momentum, estimated numbers

The early numbers are strong, with one large caveat: Meta has published no official user figures, so everything below is third-party estimation, and the estimators disagree with each other.

Muse hit #1 on the US App Store shortly after launch. Sensor Tower estimates, via Fast Company, put week-one downloads at roughly 900,000; TechCrunch, citing Sensor Tower, reported 2.8 million in about two weeks and more than 3.4 million by late September. Citi, in a note reported by Reuters on October 6, cited 6.6 million cumulative downloads and 1.8 million daily users. The WSJ, citing Sensor Tower, put global downloads at nearly 9 million by October 12. At the same late-September date, though, Apptopia counted 4.3 million downloads where Appfigures counted 2.3 million — treat all of these as ranges, not facts.

Retention cuts both ways. Sensor Tower data shows a 73% day-one return rate, almost twice ChatGPT's, but Muse churns faster after that and ChatGPT moves ahead somewhere between day 8 and day 14. Growth is also cooling: Hunterbrook Media, using TickerTrends data, estimated downloads down 8.1% week over week in early October, with net daily adds slowing sharply from the post-launch peak — though daily users still hit a record around 2.73 million on October 6. Note that Hunterbrook disclosed a short position in META, so weigh its framing accordingly.

Wonder, in brief

The models matter here mainly because they decide whether the agent works. Muse Spark, the first model out of Meta Superintelligence Labs after the $14.3 billion Scale AI deal, arrived in April and reached version 1.3 in September — credible on coding and long-context work, a step behind the frontier on agent tasks. Wonder, announced October 12 and trained at Meta's gigawatt-scale Prometheus data centre in Ohio, is the catch-up attempt, built around two ideas aimed squarely at agent work: Active Memory, carrying what matters across conversations, and Active Focus, persisting at a hard goal and checking in before consequential actions.

Read Meta's own benchmark chart with care. It is run at maximum effort, and the Wonder family leads 7 of its 14 rows — but most of those wins belong to Wonder Full, the top variant available only through Muse Code subscriptions at $50 and $200 a month. The API-accessible Wonder beats Anthropic's Opus 5.5 on only 5 of 14 rows. The independent check is more balanced: Artificial Analysis scores Wonder Full at 56 on its Intelligence Index, second tier behind Opus 5.5 at 58, with plain Wonder at 52 — a real jump from Spark 1.3's 48 in six weeks — while flagging general knowledge as the family's weak spot. On price, Wonder's API at $4 per million input tokens and $20 output exactly matches Opus 5.5 and undercuts GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 by more than half. Wonder will soon power Muse itself, subscribers first, according to Reuters and the WSJ. That is the real test: whether a better model closes the retention gap.

The bill: capex now, revenue later

Second-quarter results, reported July 29, explain the stock's split personality. Revenue was $60.80 billion, up 28% and ahead of consensus; ad revenue rose 27%. But diluted EPS of $6.18 fell 13% and missed, free cash flow collapsed 91% to $784 million, and quarterly capex hit $31.1 billion. Full-year capex guidance was narrowed to $130–145 billion — a range that has ratcheted up from $115–135 billion at the start of the year — alongside $2.4 billion of legal charges, $1.18 billion of severance, and another roughly $4.6 billion quarterly loss at Reality Labs. Crucially, Meta reported no meaningful direct AI-product revenue in the quarter; Muse launched after it ended.

So the bull case has to be built from distribution and scenarios. Meta starts with 3.60 billion daily users across its family of apps, and Meta AI already claims more than 1 billion monthly users. Citi's $27 billion-by-2030 model splits into roughly $23 billion of transaction commissions and $4.5 billion of subscriptions — a projection, not guidance. Jefferies sees $10.8 billion a year if Muse reaches 1 billion users by the end of 2027 with 3% paying, and raised its target to $875; Wells Fargo sits at $1,000 and BNP Paribas at $855. Funding the bet is not the problem: the ads engine throwing off 28% growth, with management crediting AI ranking for gains such as a 15.7% lift in Facebook ad conversions, is the strongest cash machine in consumer tech.

The bear case is arithmetic. Meta has the widest gap among the hyperscalers between capex and identifiable AI revenue, and no cloud P&L to point at — one analyst projection circulating in October, via MarketWatch, puts 2027 capex near $250 billion, well above anything the company has guided. Monetisation is unproven across the industry: PYMNTS and Bank of America Institute data suggest only about 3% of households pay for any AI service. Citi's transaction leg assumes other platforms let Muse transact on their turf, and in late September Amazon blocked Muse from making purchases, with other retailers flagging accounts. An agent whose value is acting on other companies' sites is hostage to those companies. Several firms cut targets after Q2 even while keeping Buy ratings — Evercore trimmed to $820 from $930 — and Oppenheimer downgraded outright, citing an "unknown revenue opportunity" for superintelligence spending.

Layered on top is Meta's oldest problem: trust. WIRED reported that Muse conversations are used for training unless the user opts out. Surfshark counted 31 of 35 App Store data types in Muse's disclosures, and ZDNET's testing called it the worst AI agent for privacy among those tested. In the incident that will follow this product around, Muse — operating under a seller's "Allow Always" permission — shared the seller's home address with a Marketplace buyer; Meta responded that the agent followed the permissions it was given and that it would make the prompt clearer. A September incident in which developers exported the contents of Muse's own virtual machine was, Meta says, not a breach, since users accessed only their own machine. The strongest promised fix, a Confidential VM that would cryptographically bar even Meta from user data, is slated for later in 2026 and has not shipped. Nor has the full Wonder safety report, which was still unpublished as of October 13; Meta's own model card does not rule out a high-risk rating for chemical and biological misuse without mitigations in place.

Takeaway

Muse is the first consumer agent with genuine distribution, a free tier none of its rivals match, and — as of yesterday — a near-frontier model of its own. That combination explains the rally. What it does not yet have is proof: no official user numbers, no disclosed AI revenue, retention that fades after the second week, and a $130–145 billion annual capex bill that is very much official. The next checkpoint is third-quarter earnings in late October — the first quarter containing Muse, and the first chance for Meta to replace estimates with numbers. Until then, META is a leveraged bet that the front door to the internet is worth more than the house being built behind it.
