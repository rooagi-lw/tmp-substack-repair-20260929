The Nasdaq fell 4.48% and chip stocks lost 11.41% in the worst week for technology since April 2025 — yet the Dow gained, consumer staples rose 5%, and the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 0.5% while the cap-weighted index fell 1.95%, per Morningstar. Hot September CPI and an even hotter PPI should have been a sell-everything week. Instead it was a sell-AI week: investors questioned how the buildout is being paid for — CoreWeave's bonds yielded 16% — and moved the money into Coca-Cola, Walmart and healthcare. The rotation, not the decline, is the story.

Where the week was won and lost

Seven of the eleven S&P 500 sectors finished the week higher, per Seeking Alpha's weekly tally. The winners were the corners the AI trade had ignored: consumer staples (+5.0%) and energy (+5.0%) tied for the lead, followed by healthcare (+3.4%), materials (+2.5%), utilities (+2.0%), financials (+1.4%) and real estate (+1.1%). Inside staples, Coca-Cola rose 6.64%, Walmart 6.70% and Procter & Gamble 5.37% on the week, per Yahoo Finance sector data cited by Portfolio Terminal, and healthcare's XLV closed at a record $177.19 on Thursday.

The loser was singular and enormous: technology (-6.4%), its biggest weekly loss since April 2025, per Dow Jones Market Data via Morningstar, with the sector's SPDR fund down 7.70%. Consumer discretionary (-1.9%), communication services (-1.6%) and industrials (-0.9%) also fell. The gap between the best sector and technology was roughly 11 to 13 percentage points depending on the measure — nearly triple the week before. As Interactive Brokers' Steve Sosnick told MarketWatch: "Nobody really wants to be out of the market. If people were really genuinely concerned, they'd be selling and not rushing to buy something else."

The watchlist: what the week's tape surfaced

Setups worth watching — each with the thesis and what would break it. For reference only, not investment advice.

Potential buys:

  • The defensive rotation (XLP, XLV; KO, WMT, PG, UNH). Staples had their best week since February and healthcare set a record close, per Portfolio Terminal and Morningstar, while UnitedHealth beat earnings and raised guidance (see the scorecard below). Thesis: money leaving AI is parking in cash-flow certainty, not leaving equities — a rotation that has historically been healthier than a dash to cash. Breaks if: next week's megacap earnings reassert the AI trade so completely that the rotation unwinds in days.
  • Energy (XLE, +5.0%). WTI settled above $100 for the first time in this cycle (see Energy and gold below) and Brent added a Hormuz risk premium, per ING strategists cited by TS2. Thesis: energy is both the week's momentum and its inflation hedge. Breaks if: crude gives back the $100 level and the inflation scare fades with it.
  • The quality banks (GS, C, MS). Goldman earned $17.01 a share against roughly $13 expected, Citi beat, and Morgan Stanley beat on equity trading, per Reuters — trading revenue thrives on exactly this volatility. Thesis: the toll-takers win whichever way the AI trade breaks. Breaks if: credit costs — Wells Fargo's provisions jumped 51% — spread from a footnote to the headline.

Potential sells / shorts:

  • Optical networking and AI infrastructure (CIEN -26%, COHR -26%, LITE -25%, HPE -25% on the week, per Seeking Alpha). The epicenter of the rout: the names that supply the buildout fell a quarter in five sessions as the market started pricing the financing, not just the demand. Thesis: momentum is broken and the group's multiples assumed flawless funding. Breaks if: Meta and Tesla's Wednesday earnings reaffirm capex at full throttle.
  • The neocloud credit trade (CRWV). CoreWeave fell more than 15% on the week, its 2031 bond yield briefly surged past 16%, and the cost of insuring its roughly $50 billion of debt against default passed 1,000 basis points to record highs, per Reuters and S&P Global Market Intelligence. A Gartner warning that most neocloud providers will not survive independently by 2030 added fuel, per Stocktwits. Thesis: this is now a credit story, and credit stories do not bottom on equity dip-buying. Breaks if: the bonds recover — watch the debt, not the stock.
  • Software (IGV -7.4% on the week, per MarketWatch and IBD). Friday's leg down hit the group investors had treated as the safer side of tech: Twilio fell 10.6% Friday, the S&P 500's worst that day, while CrowdStrike and Snowflake each lost about 17% on the week and Palo Alto Networks and Cloudflare about 18%, per IBD. Thesis: there is no hiding inside tech when the theme itself is being repriced. Breaks if: Wednesday's megacap prints stabilize the whole complex.

The scoreboard

Friday was the rout's exclamation point: the S&P 500 fell 1.09% to 7,659.44, the Nasdaq dropped 2.14% to 26,140.03, the Dow slipped 0.34% to 52,002.41 and the Russell 2000 fell 1.66% to 2,721.37, per the Wall Street Journal and Investopedia. The VIX jumped 21% on the day to 19.99, its highest close since July 29.

The week split the market in two. The S&P 500 lost 1.95% — its biggest weekly drop since June 26, per Morningstar — and the Nasdaq lost 4.48%, snapping a four-week winning streak, per Investopedia. The Dow rose 0.67%, its second straight weekly gain, and the Russell 2000 fell 3.05%, per Seeking Alpha. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 11.41% on the week, per Portfolio Terminal. Monday was a market holiday in bonds only: Columbus Day left equities open with the Treasury market closed and liquidity thin. For the year, the scoreboard still reads green: S&P 500 +11.9%, Nasdaq +12.5%, Dow +8.2%, Russell 2000 +9.6%, per Portfolio Terminal's weekly close data.

And the breadth underneath kept improving even as the indexes fell: the equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP) rose 0.5% on the week, per Morningstar — the average stock gained in a week the index lost nearly 2%. That is last week's broadening, surviving its first real stress test.

The bond revolt

Bonds did the opposite of what the inflation data ordered. The 10-year Treasury yield spiked to 5.33% after Wednesday's CPI, per Investopedia, then reversed as the tech selloff drove a flight to safety, finishing Friday at 5.098% — down about 15 basis points on the week from 5.244%, its biggest weekly pullback since February, per Dow Jones Market Data via Morningstar. The 2-year ended Friday at 4.717%, per Summa Money's closing summary.

Read that again: the two hottest inflation prints of the year landed on Wednesday and Thursday, and yields fell on the week. The bond market is no longer trading the next CPI — it is trading the risk that 5%-plus money breaks something leveraged, and this week the something had a name: AI debt. When CoreWeave's bonds trade at 16%, Treasuries at 5.1% start to look like the safe asset again. The revolt has not ended; it has changed targets.

The data that moved the Fed

Act one, Wednesday: September CPI rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% from a year earlier, up from 3.4% in August, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Gasoline jumped 9.1% and accounted for more than half the monthly increase; the energy index rose 5.2% and shelter 0.4%. Core CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% year over year, above the 0.2% and 2.5% consensus, per BLS data and pre-release surveys. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack had warned on Monday that she sees inflation pressures as "relatively broad-based," with demand-side pressure from the AI buildout itself, per reporting on her October 12 remarks.

Act two, Thursday: September PPI rose 1.3% on the month and 6.3% year over year — the largest monthly jump since March 2022 and the biggest annual gain since December 2022, according to BLS — against expectations of roughly 0.5% on the month. Energy goods surged 10.8%, with wholesale gasoline up 20.3%, and core PPI rose 0.7% on the month, so the pressure was not only at the pump. The same morning, September retail sales rose 0.8%, beating the roughly 0.3–0.4% consensus — but excluding gas stations the gain was just 0.2%, and the control group rose only 0.1%, per the Commerce Department data cited by Babypips and Continuum Economics. The consumer is spending; mostly, the consumer is paying for fuel. Friday added import prices up 1.1% and industrial production up 0.7%, both above consensus, per eOption's data summary.

Act three, the Fed's response: silence, deliberately. Chair Kevin Warsh, speaking overnight at an IMF meeting in Bangkok, refused to signal the next move — "It's a new chapter at the Fed, and we're not in that business anymore," per Investopedia. Traders nonetheless marked October down and December up: CME FedWatch put a hike at the October 27–28 meeting at 17%, down from 28% a day earlier, while a December hike sat at 89%, unchanged, per Investopedia (other trackers put October lower still, around 10–16%). A week ago the read was roughly 18% for October and about 80% for a year-end rate of 4.00–4.25% or higher. The market's verdict: the Fed will not hike into a tech rout, but it will hike into this inflation by December.

Energy and gold

WTI crude settled Friday at $101.84 a barrel, up 10.9% on the week, and Brent settled at $109.86, up 4.9%, per Seeking Alpha and Reuters — Brent rose 3.7% on Friday alone as markets priced a significant risk premium around the Strait of Hormuz, per ING. Oil is now both the cause of the inflation prints and the hedge against them, which is why energy shares could rise 5% in a week the S&P 500 fell 2%.

Gold went nowhere and said plenty doing it: front-month futures ended at about $4,186.70 an ounce, down 0.7% on the week, per Seeking Alpha, with spot near $4,166 on Friday, per Trading Economics via FinanceFeeds. A hot CPI, a falling dollar (the index eased to about 101.84) and a volatility spike should have been gold's week. That it merely held its ground near $4,200 says the haven bid went to Treasuries instead.

Earnings scorecard: this week's winners and losers

Winner: Goldman Sachs (GS). The quarter's blowout. Earnings of $17.01 per share against roughly $13.02 expected (LSEG consensus, per TheStreet), on net revenue of $18.68 billion, up 23% year over year, with net income up 29% to $5.29 billion, per the company's results as reported by Reuters and multiple outlets. The engine was equities trading revenue, up 72% to $6.44 billion, and investment banking fees up 21% to $3.22 billion. Volatility is Goldman's product, and this market is supplying it. Runner-up: UnitedHealth (UNH) — adjusted EPS of $4.69 versus $4.12 expected on revenue of $112.7 billion, with full-year adjusted guidance raised to at least $20.50 per share, per the company's October 13 release.

Loser: JPMorgan (JPM). A rare double miss from the industry's benchmark: reported EPS of $5.57 versus $5.93 expected (LSEG) and revenue of $50.2 billion versus $51.23 billion expected, per FinanceFeeds' summary of the release — with $658 million of pretax losses on investment securities flagged in the quarter. Net interest income of about $27 billion edged past expectations, which made the headline miss sting more, not less. Dishonorable mention: Bank of America (BAC), which missed on both earnings ($1.08 versus $1.10 expected) and revenue and fell more than 3% premarket, per Everhint's earnings summary, after booking a roughly $1.2 billion regulatory settlement charge.

The beat the market sold: TSMC (TSM). Record results, sold anyway. Third-quarter revenue of $46.72 billion beat the roughly $45.62 billion consensus, EPS of $4.60 per ADR beat the $4.42 expected, gross margin hit 66.4%, and management guided fourth-quarter revenue to $52.3–54.3 billion, per TSMC's official release. The shares still fell — down about 2% in premarket on the print and caught in the week's chip washout, per TradingKey. When the best-positioned company in AI posts a sixth straight record profit and the stock cannot rise, the problem is not the fundamentals; it is the price already paid for them. The same pattern, smaller: Citi beat and its shares fell, and Domino's beat on both lines and dropped about 4%, per Everhint.

The takeaway: the indexes fell and the market got healthier and more fragile at the same time — healthier because seven sectors rose and the average stock gained, more fragile because the leadership being sold is the leadership that carries the index's earnings and its debt. Yields falling on hot inflation is the bond market saying the risk has moved from prices to credit. Watch CoreWeave's bonds and Meta's capex line. They are the same trade now.
