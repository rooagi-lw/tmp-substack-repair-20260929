Nike and Lululemon jumped together on Thursday morning, and neither company had announced anything. Nike rose 4.6% to $36.91 and Lululemon rose 3.6% to $97.50 as of about 9:50 a.m. ET, on a morning when semiconductor stocks were falling for a seventh straight session and money was rotating into defensive and beaten-down consumer names. The two stocks are, in effect, one sector — premium athletic apparel — and today the sector got a relief bid that landed hardest on its two most de-rated, most heavily shorted names.

The move

Both stocks came into Thursday near the bottom of their ranges. Nike closed Wednesday at $35.30, down 42% year to date, with a 52-week range of $31.97 to $70.44. Lululemon closed at $94.09, down 53% year to date, and was trading only about 8% above its 52-week low of $90.38 (range $90.38 to $225.98).

The rest of the group moved the same way, in proportion to how beaten down it was:

  • Crocs (CROX) +3.6% to about $121.80 — though Crocs is up 43% year to date, so a broad footwear bid was also present
  • Under Armour (UAA) +2.3% to $4.90
  • Deckers (DECK) +2.0% to about $84.50, down 18% year to date
  • VF Corp (VFC) +1.3% to $16.69, after already re-rating over the past month
  • Levi's (LEVI) +1.0% to $19.77
  • On Holding (ONON) -0.6% to $35.29 — the exception. On is the group's premium growth name, up about 30% in the last month after its investor day and buyback news, and it sat this move out.

The context benchmarks tell the same story: the SPDR S&P Retail ETF (XRT) rose 2.3%, Walmart rose 3.6%, and MarketWatch had Nike and Walmart as the Dow's two strongest contributors in early trading — while the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH) fell 3.2% and Nvidia fell 3.0%.

Why today: three things, ranked

1. Moody's flipped the sector outlook. Moody's Ratings raised its global retail and apparel outlook to stable from negative in a report dated October 7, 2026, citing easing tariff costs and stable labor markets (Retail Dive, October 14). The agency now forecasts sector revenue growth of 2% to 4% and adjusted EBIT growth of 0% to 2% over the next 12 to 18 months, versus an EBIT decline in 2025 — and it specifically noted that tariff refunds could help offset higher energy and transport costs. The report is a week old, but its coverage landed this week, and its thesis fits Nike and Lululemon better than anyone: both are among the sector's heaviest tariff payers, and both have already booked nine-figure refunds.

2. September retail sales beat, right before the open. US retail sales rose 0.8% in September against expectations of about 0.3%, with August revised up to 1.3% (Reuters, October 15). The print landed at 8:30 a.m. ET, an hour before the open — timing-perfect for a morning retail bid. The honest caveat: most of the increase was gasoline, with sales excluding gas stations up only 0.2% (Wall Street Journal), and the Federal Reserve's Beige Book described consumer spending as little changed. This was a headline bid more than a fundamental consumer upgrade.

3. Tariff refunds keep gaining legal momentum. On October 8, a US Court of International Trade judge certified a class covering importers who paid the IEEPA tariffs and had not filed their own suits (Bloomberg Law, October 14). The Supreme Court ruled those tariffs unlawful in February 2026; the total refund pool is roughly $166 billion, and trade press reports more than $126 billion has already been returned through Customs' CAPE portal. The proof is showing up in apparel earnings: Levi Strauss raised its full-year profit forecast on October 7 after receiving $79 million of refunds in its latest quarter (Reuters). For Nike and Lululemon specifically the incremental money is limited — Nike already booked about $986 million in its June quarter and Lululemon already received $134.5 million — but each new development keeps the refund narrative, and the sector re-rating, alive.

Underneath all three sits the rotation: September CPI, released October 14 by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, came in hot at +0.6% on the month and +3.7% on the year, semiconductors fell for a sixth straight session on October 14 (the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 4.3%), and healthcare and staples each rose more than 2%. Thursday morning continued the pattern — out of crowded AI winners, into defensives and de-rated consumer names.

What it wasn't

Days like this attract recycled stories, and today had a prominent one. Reports circulating this morning that CEO Elliott Hill bought about $1 million of Nike stock and director Tim Cook bought 50,000 shares (about $3 million) are old news, twice over. Cook's 50,000-share purchase at $58.97 was made on December 22, 2025, after the December earnings drop; Hill bought about $1 million on December 29, 2025. A second, smaller round followed in April 2026 — Cook bought 25,000 shares at $42.43 on April 10 and Hill about $1 million at $42.27 on April 13. No October 2026 insider purchase appears in any Form 4. The two genuine rounds of insider buying do support a background "insiders bought the dip" story — but they are not today's catalyst.

Also not catalysts:

  • Analyst actions. No upgrade or target raise for either stock was published October 13–15. The recent flow was bearish: Berenberg reiterated its Sell rating and $27.50 target on Nike on October 14, UBS reiterated Hold on October 13, and Lululemon's last wave of actions was a set of September target cuts that left consensus at Reduce.
  • The RTFKT exit. Nike sold its NFT venture RTFKT in December 2025, not this month.
  • A Converse sale. Bloomberg reported in March 2026 that Authentic Brands Group would be interested if Converse were for sale; Nike has not engaged and has announced no review.
  • China data. China's September retail sales and third-quarter GDP are not due until October 19. The latest hard data, August retail sales up 0.4% year over year (Reuters), was weak.

The shared setup

Nike and Lululemon moved together because they share the same four exposures:

  • The same customer problem. Both are premium-priced, North-America-heavy athletic brands losing momentum at the same time — Nike's lifestyle franchises (Dunk, Jordan Retro) are shrinking, partly by design, while Lululemon's core leggings business fell about 20% in its latest quarter and Americas comparable sales fell 12%.
  • China as the swing market, currently a drag for both. Nike's Greater China revenue fell 22% in its latest quarter; Lululemon China's growth slowed to 4% (down 2% in constant currency).
  • Asia-manufacturing tariff exposure. Nike sources roughly half its footwear from Vietnam; Lululemon has guided to $380 million of gross tariff costs for 2026. Every tariff-easing or refund headline is worth more to these two than to almost anyone else in retail.
  • Trough valuations after guidance resets. Both companies cut guidance hard within the last six weeks — Lululemon on September 3, Nike on October 1 — and both trade far below their historical multiples.

Nike by the numbers

Nike's first quarter of fiscal 2027 (ended August 31, 2026; reported October 1) showed a business split in two. Revenue was $11.2 billion, down 4%, a slight miss against consensus of about $11.3 billion, while diluted EPS of $0.48 beat the roughly $0.43 consensus (Nike press release, October 1). Gross margin improved to 42.8%, up 60 basis points. North America revenue rose 2% to $5.13 billion with wholesale up 9%, and the performance portfolio — running, football, basketball — grew at high-single to double-digit rates. Against that, Greater China fell 22% to $1.18 billion (down 26% currency-neutral, its ninth straight quarterly decline), NIKE Direct fell 8% with digital down 13%, and Converse fell 28% to $263 million.

Guidance is the problem. Nike guided fiscal 2027 revenue to a high-single-digit decline and adjusted EPS to $1.15–$1.35, well below the roughly $1.65–1.69 expected, with China expected to deteriorate further. A new cost program, Pace, targets about $2.5 billion of cumulative savings through fiscal 2031, but most of the savings arrive in fiscal 2029–30. At $36.91 the stock trades at about 16.8 times trailing earnings — roughly half its five-year median multiple, though that trailing figure is flattered by the $986 million refund booked in the June quarter; on its own guided fiscal 2027 EPS, the multiple is in the high 20s. The dividend yield is about 4.6%. The next major catalyst is Investor Day on November 16–17, where new CFO Dave Denton has promised a five-year financial framework.

Lululemon by the numbers

Lululemon's second quarter of fiscal 2026 (ended August 2; reported September 3) was weaker. Net revenue fell 4% to $2.42 billion, missing consensus of about $2.46 billion, and comparable sales fell 9%, with Americas comps down 12% (company results, September 3). Reported EPS of $2.92 looked strong against consensus, but $0.86 of it was the $134.5 million IEEPA refund plus interest; excluding the refund, EPS was about $2.06, down from $3.10 a year earlier. Management cut full-year guidance to revenue of $10.35–10.50 billion (down 5% to 7%) and EPS of $9.48–$9.73, from $10.95–$11.15 previously — against $13.26 earned in fiscal 2025.

The leadership reset is the background story. Calvin McDonald stepped down on January 31, 2026; Heidi O'Neill, a 27-year Nike veteran, took over as CEO on September 8. On October 8 she announced her first overhaul — a new President & Chief Product Officer (Maggie Gauger, from Athleta and before that 23 years at Nike) and a new COO, both starting October 26 — which Jefferies called a step in the right direction. One loose end: the June 2026 settlement with founder Chip Wilson required a third, mutually agreed director to be appointed by October 1, 2026, and no announcement naming that director has appeared. At $97.50, Lululemon trades at about 7.6 times trailing earnings and roughly 10 times its own guided fiscal 2026 EPS — the cheaper of the two on forward numbers. There is no dividend.

Positioning: why relief moves these two so far

The short trade was crowded and still growing into September. As of the September 30 settlement (MarketBeat), Nike short interest was 99.24 million shares — 6.76% of the float, up 15.8% in September alone, worth about $3.51 billion — and S3 Partners described the roughly 87 million shares short earlier in the month as a record, up about 55 million shares in a year. Lululemon's short interest was 15.38 million shares, 13.35% of the float, up 11.6% in September. Bears were pressing winners rather than covering, which is the classic setup for a sharp bounce on any sector-positive headline: incremental buying meets a crowded exit. No direct short-covering data for Thursday morning was available by mid-morning — the covering read rests on the price pattern, with the two most-shorted, worst-performing names leading and the sector's recent winner flat.

Bottom line

Nothing changed at Nike or Lululemon on Thursday. What changed was the narrative around the sector they share: Moody's decided tariff pain is easing, refunds are turning into real cash across apparel P&Ls, a retail-sales beat gave the bid a morning trigger, and two crowded shorts did the rest. Our confidence that this is a sector-and-positioning move rather than company news is about 65% — it would rise with closing attribution from major outlets and fall if a company-specific filing or upgrade surfaces after the fact. What to watch next: China's September data on October 19, Nike's Investor Day on November 16–17, and whether Lululemon names its third settlement director.
