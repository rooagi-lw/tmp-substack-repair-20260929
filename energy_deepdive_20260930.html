Deep Dive: Energy — Oil's $100 Tug-of-War

On Tuesday, a tanker crossing the Strait of Hormuz was struck by an unknown projectile. Nobody has claimed responsibility. By Wednesday morning, Brent crude was back above $103 a barrel. Seven months after American and Israeli bombs first fell on Iran, a single explosion in a 21-mile-wide waterway still sets the price of everything — and the connections running underneath the headlines are the real story.

The week that was

Tuesday opened with what looked like a breakthrough. Axios reported that President Trump was prepared to offer Tehran sanctions relief and release frozen Iranian funds in exchange for concrete steps on its nuclear program — the kind of deal that could let Iranian crude flow back onto world markets. Within hours, Trump denied it on social media, calling the report untrue and saying he had offered Iran nothing, per Oilprice.com. Crude jumped: Brent rose 0.8% to about $103.40 a barrel, WTI gained 0.3% to about $89.60.

The same day brought a darker headline. UK Maritime Trade Operations issued a warning Wednesday that a tanker transiting the Strait of Hormuz had been hit by an unknown projectile on Tuesday, per the Wall Street Journal — source verified, authorities investigating, no one claiming it. Meanwhile Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis stepped up attacks in the Red Sea and asserted control over the Bab al-Mandeb Strait, per the Journal — the very corridor shippers use to bypass Hormuz.

By Wednesday, the market had made up its mind. December Brent futures rose 2.6% to $98.66, front-month WTI gained 2.2% to $89.18, both reversing early losses, per the Journal. Behind the scenes, Qatar kept shuttling messages between Washington and Tehran, "working towards establishing common ground," in Doha's words — the quiet channel that could end the whole thing, or collapse trying.

How we got here

On February 28, 2026, the US and Israel launched "Operation Epic Fury" — a surprise air campaign striking more than 900 targets across Iran: nuclear facilities, military leadership, missile production. The first strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, according to contemporaneous news reports. Iran's Revolutionary Guard answered within hours by declaring the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed — boarding merchant ships, attacking vessels, laying sea mines across a waterway that carried about 25% of the world's seaborne oil trade and 20% of its liquefied natural gas.

What followed was the fastest oil shock in modern history. Brent crossed $100 on March 8 — the first time in four years — and peaked at $126, the largest-ever monthly price increase, in what became the biggest energy-supply disruption since the 1970s crisis. The US blockaded Iranian ports from April 13 to May 29 while Pakistan mediated ceasefire talks; the International Maritime Organization counted roughly 2,000 stranded ships and 20,000 stranded mariners in the Gulf.

A Switzerland-brokered memorandum paused the fighting for two months starting in June. It expired in August, and the war resumed. September brought a fragile normalcy: Kpler data puts Hormuz traffic at 13.2 million barrels a day — 77% of pre-war levels, per CNBC (Reuters cited a lower 7.4 million figure). But as Tuesday's tanker attack showed, 77% of normal is not normal. "Despite more vessel traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, flows remain below pre-conflict levels, keeping the market undersupplied," per UBS commodity analyst Giovanni Staunovo.

The hidden connections

1. The dollar-gold-oil triangle. Oil doesn't move alone — it moves against the dollar, and alongside gold. Gold sits near $4,180 an ounce, up 1.5% on Tuesday: when war risk rises, money buys both barrels and bullion, and the two great fear gauges are flashing the same signal. But the dollar is arguing the opposite. Crude and gold are both priced in dollars worldwide, so a firm greenback normally caps both — it makes oil pricier for foreign buyers, denting demand, and raises the bar for gold. The dollar index rose 0.3% on Tuesday, yet both commodities held near their highs anyway: fear is overpowering the currency drag. Stranger still, the 10-year Treasury at 5.26% — the highest since 2002 — should be crushing gold, which pays nothing to hold. It isn't, which measures just how powerful the safe-haven bid is. The catch: they unwind together. If Qatar's mediation produces even a partial deal, the war premium comes out of oil and the fear bid comes out of gold at the same time. If the Fed softens — softer August PCE is already cooling October hike odds — a weaker dollar lifts both at once.

2. The midterm clock. This war is now a domestic political story. Every $10 in crude is a tax on American drivers, five weeks before the midterm elections — and higher energy costs are already a noted headache for Republicans, per the Times of India. Watch Trump's shifting posture through that lens: "unconditional surrender" in March, shifting goals and timelines since, denying sanctions relief this week. Oil price creates political pressure, political pressure creates unpredictable policy, unpredictable policy moves oil. The triangle closes on itself.

3. The winners nobody talks about. Everyone watches ExxonMobil ($164.31, +1.8% Wednesday, per Finnhub) and Chevron ($206.71, +1.1%). But the war redistributes profit across the whole chain. Refiners like Marathon and Valero live on crude spreads that swing violently with each headline. Airlines and chemical makers breathe easier with every supply story that pushes crude down. And while the world watched the fighting, tanker owners and marine insurers quietly made fortunes on war-risk premiums — pricing danger by the voyage while 2,000 ships sat stranded.

4. The LNG shadow. Hormuz carried about a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas, most of it from Qatar. Europe and Asia's winter heating bills are hostage to the same strait as the oil market — this was never just a crude story. Any escalation that touches LNG flows reprices energy on two continents at once.

5. The OPEC+ paradox. OPEC+ is about to flood the market because prices are high — expected to discuss fast-tracking the remaining 1.66 million barrels a day of its cuts in three monthly tranches of ~500,000 bpd starting November, per reports citing an OPEC delegate, part of restoring 2.2 million bpd by September 2026. But if they succeed, they kill the price that justified it. Note the quiet signal inside the noise: Saudi Arabia exported 5.8 million bpd in September, the most since February, per ANZ Research — Riyadh pumping hard while a war rages next door is a choice of market share over price. Against that, the IEA now expects a record 3.33 million bpd global surplus next year, and Iraq's deal to restart Kurdish pipeline exports could add another 500,000 bpd. The cartel is racing the war premium — and the premium is winning, for now.

Two traders, one screen

The bull sees a risk premium that is earned, not imagined. A tanker was literally hit this week. The Houthis are widening the conflict zone. Flows are still a quarter below normal, and "continued uncertainty over sanctions relief and negotiations is keeping a geopolitical risk premium embedded in prices," says SS WealthStreet's Sugandha Sachdeva, as quoted by Reuters. Capital Economics sees near-term risks skewed to the upside, forecasting Brent at $100 at year-end.

The bear sees supply coming like a freight train. OPEC+ fast-tracking 1.5 million bpd, Iraq's 500,000 bpd, a record IEA surplus, Indian crude imports down 2.9% year-on-year — and Capital Economics at $70 Brent by end-2027 as the market loosens. If Doha's quiet channel produces even a partial deal, the war premium unwinds violently, and gold falls with it.

What to watch next

  1. OPEC+ meeting (Sunday): Will the group formally fast-track the three 500,000 bpd tranches starting November?
  2. Qatar's shuttle: Any sign of common ground between Washington and Tehran — a breakthrough reprices crude and gold down fast.
  3. Hormuz shipping: The UKMTO investigation into Tuesday's strike; another hit keeps the premium bid.
  4. November midterms: The political clock — sustained high gasoline prices cut against incumbents, and incumbents under pressure make unpredictable policy.

Takeaway: Oil isn't trading on supply and demand right now — it's trading on war headlines versus OPEC+ taps, with the dollar, gold, and the midterm clock pulling the strings underneath. Whichever side blinks first decides whether Brent sees $110 or $80 — and it won't move alone.
