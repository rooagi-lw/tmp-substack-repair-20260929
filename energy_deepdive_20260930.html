Deep Dive: Energy — Oil's $100 Tug-of-War

Seven months after American and Israeli bombs shut the world's most important oil waterway, crude is stuck in a standoff: every headline about Iran pushes it toward $110, and every OPEC+ headline drags it back toward $80.

What happened

  • 🛢️ Trump kills sanctions-relief hopes. Axios reported the White House was prepared to ease sanctions on Iran and release frozen Iranian funds in exchange for nuclear concessions. President Trump denied it on social media, saying the report was untrue and he had offered Tehran nothing, per Oilprice.com. Brent rose 0.8% to about $103.40 a barrel; WTI gained 0.3% to about $89.60.
  • 🚢 A tanker was hit in the Strait of Hormuz. A vessel transiting the strait was struck by an unknown projectile on Tuesday, according to a warning issued Wednesday by UK Maritime Trade Operations, per the Wall Street Journal. Authorities are investigating.
  • 🔥 Houthis escalate in the Red Sea. Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi militia has stepped up attacks in the Red Sea and asserted control over the Bab al-Mandeb Strait, a corridor that helps bypass Hormuz, per the Wall Street Journal.
  • 📈 Prices bounced Wednesday. December Brent futures rose 2.6% to $98.66 and front-month WTI gained 2.2% to $89.18 on Wednesday, reversing earlier losses, per the Wall Street Journal.

How we got here

  • February 28, 2026: The US and Israel launched "Operation Epic Fury," a massive air campaign against Iran hitting more than 900 targets — nuclear facilities, military leadership, missile production — according to contemporaneous news reports. Iran's Revolutionary Guard answered by declaring the Strait of Hormuz, which carried about 25% of the world's seaborne oil trade and 20% of its LNG, effectively closed: boarding ships, attacking vessels, laying mines.
  • March: Brent blew past $100 for the first time in four years and peaked at $126 — the largest-ever monthly price increase — in what became the biggest oil-supply disruption since the 1970s energy crisis.
  • April–May: The US blockaded Iranian ports (April 13–May 29) while ceasefire talks ran through Pakistani mediators. The International Maritime Organization reported about 2,000 ships and 20,000 mariners stranded in the Gulf.
  • June–August: A Switzerland-brokered memorandum paused the fighting for two months; it expired in August and the conflict resumed.
  • September: Flows are recovering — Kpler data puts Hormuz traffic at 13.2 million barrels a day, 77% of pre-war levels, per CNBC (Reuters cited a lower 7.4 million figure) — but "despite more vessel traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, flows remain below pre-conflict levels, keeping the market undersupplied," per UBS commodity analyst Giovanni Staunovo.

By the numbers

  • $103 / $89.60: Brent and WTI today — Brent is up roughly $10 this month, WTI about $3, per Oilprice.com.
  • 13.2M bpd: Hormuz flows recovered to 77% of pre-war levels, per Kpler via CNBC.
  • 5.8M bpd: Saudi crude exports in September, the highest since February, per ANZ Research — with East-West pipeline flows recovering faster than expected.
  • 28.55M bpd: OPEC's August production, up 400,000 bpd to the highest in over two years.
  • 3 × 500,000 bpd: OPEC+ is expected to discuss fast-tracking the remaining 1.66 million bpd of its supply cuts in three monthly installments starting November, per reports citing an OPEC delegate — part of restoring 2.2 million bpd by September 2026.
  • 3.33M bpd: The record global oil surplus the IEA now expects next year, about 360,000 bpd more than it projected a month ago.
  • +39% YTD: The XLE energy ETF ($62.08, per Finnhub), with ExxonMobil ($164.31, +1.8% today) and Chevron ($206.71, +1.1%) each up more than 35% this year.

Why it matters

Energy is the market's inflation engine. With US midterm elections five weeks out, gasoline prices are a live political variable — higher energy costs are already pressuring consumers and the broader economy, a noted headache for Republicans. For investors, the sector is a hedge and a trap at once: XLE is up nearly 40% this year, but every dollar of that gain rests on a war premium that diplomacy could erase in a weekend. Refiners, airlines, and chemical makers sit on the other side of the trade — their margins expand with every supply headline that pushes crude down.

Bull vs. bear

The bull case: The risk premium is earned, not imagined. A tanker was literally hit this week, the Houthis are widening the conflict zone, and flows are still a quarter below normal. "Continued uncertainty over sanctions relief and negotiations is keeping a geopolitical risk premium embedded in prices," says SS WealthStreet's Sugandha Sachdeva, as quoted by Reuters. Capital Economics sees near-term risks skewed to the upside and forecasts Brent at $100 at year-end.

The bear case: Supply is coming, fast. OPEC+ is about to fast-track 1.5 million bpd back onto the market, Iraq's Kurdish pipeline deal could add another 500,000 bpd, the IEA sees a record 3.33 million bpd surplus next year, and Indian crude imports just fell 2.9% year-on-year. Capital Economics expects Brent at $70 by end-2027 as the market loosens. If Qatar's mediation produces even a partial deal, the war premium unwinds violently.

What to watch next

  1. OPEC+ meeting (Sunday): Will the group formally fast-track the three 500,000 bpd tranches starting November?
  2. Qatar-mediated talks: Any sign of common ground between Washington and Tehran — a breakthrough reprices crude down fast.
  3. Hormuz shipping: The UKMTO investigation into Tuesday's tanker strike; another hit keeps the premium bid.
  4. November midterms: The political clock — sustained high gasoline prices cut against incumbents.

Takeaway: Oil isn't trading on supply and demand right now — it's trading on war headlines versus OPEC+ taps, and whichever side blinks first decides whether Brent sees $110 or $80.
