Friday's rally fixed the headlines but not the week. The Nasdaq closed at a record while the S&P 500 and Dow finished red, the equal-weight index fell for a seventh straight week, and the bond market priced the exact opposite of stocks — a 10-year yield at a 24-year high that says inflation isn't done. The week's real argument isn't about jobs. It's about whether the long end of the curve will let the Fed pause.

Where the week was won and lost

Only 3 of the 11 S&P 500 sectors finished the week positive; 8 finished red, per Seeking Alpha's weekly tally. The winners: technology (+1.9%) — the XLK's sixth straight weekly gain and its first record close since June, per MarketWatch — and energy (+1.2%), lifted by export-ban headlines and fuel-shortage fears. The losers: healthcare (-2.6%), the week's worst performer as the rally rotated out of defensives, and financial services (-2.5%), per Morningstar. Only 35% of the 872 US-listed companies Morningstar tracks rose on the week; 65% fell.

The scoreboard

Friday looked like a celebration: the S&P 500 rose 0.7% to 7,722.72, within 1% of its all-time high; the Nasdaq jumped 1.2% to 27,190.86, setting a new intraday record; the Dow added 0.5% to 51,176.96; the Russell 2000 gained 0.9%, its best day in a month, per Barchart's closing recap. Every S&P 500 sector except Health Care finished green.

The week told a different story. The S&P 500 fell 0.3% — its fourth weekly decline in five weeks. The Dow dropped 1.3%. The Russell 2000 slipped 0.2%. Only the Nasdaq managed a gain, up 0.5%, its fifth weekly advance in six. For the year, the scoreboard still shines: S&P 500 +12.8%, Nasdaq +17%, Russell 2000 +14.1%, Dow +6.5%.

And beneath even that, the narrowest signal of all: the equal-weight S&P 500 fell for a seventh consecutive week even as cap-weighted tech hit records — tied for its longest weekly losing streak since the May 2022 bear market, per MarketWatch. Through Wednesday, NYSE stocks making new 52-week lows had outnumbered new highs for 23 straight sessions, the longest such stretch since October 2023, and the NYSE advance-decline line kept trending lower. When the average stock keeps falling while the index holds up, that is a concentration warning, not a bull-market confirmation.

The bond revolt

If equities spent the week debating the Fed, bonds spent it ignoring the debate. The 10-year Treasury yield closed Friday at 5.28% — after dipping to 5.16% in the first minutes after the jobs report, then retracing the whole move as oil recovered. On Thursday it had touched nearly 5.35%, a fresh 24-year high, per Investopedia's market coverage.

The shape of the move matters more than the level. The 2-year yield barely moved on the week — 4.81% to 4.83% — while the 10-year jumped 11 basis points, 5.17% to 5.28%, per Morningstar's weekly update. The long end sold off while the front end stayed anchored: a bear steepening that points to inflation plus fiscal and term-premium pressure, not to stronger real growth. Thursday's ISM Manufacturing report added fuel: the headline held at 54.5, a ninth straight month of expansion, but the Prices Paid component surged 6.8 points to 77.9 — its highest since the Iran conflict began — with not a single commodity category reporting a price decrease.

This is the week's hidden connection: stocks rallied on the idea that the Fed is done, while bonds priced the idea that inflation isn't. Both can be right at once — the Fed can pause on a weak labor market while long-end yields stay elevated on energy, deficits, and term premium. That divergence, strong equities alongside elevated long-term yields, is the most consequential cross-asset signal heading into October.

The data that moved the Fed

It was a genuine three-act week for rate expectations. Act one, Wednesday: August core PCE came in at 3.0% year over year, well below the 3.3% forecast — October hike probability collapsed from 71% to 38% in a single session, Goldman Sachs pushed its next-hike call from October to December, and NY Fed President John Williams said the central bank has time and signaled no urgency. The same day's third estimate of Q2 GDP revised growth up to 2.2% annualized from 1.5%, a meaningful counterpoint.

Act two, Friday: September payrolls added just 29,000 jobs versus ~90,000 expected, unemployment ticked to 4.2%, and prior months were revised down 60,000. By the close, traders priced only a 23% chance of an October hike — down from 64% one week earlier, per CME FedWatch. The VIX fell 6% to 15.40 on the relief.

The catch, as ever: weak hiring is good news for valuations until it becomes bad news for sales and profits.

Energy, gold, and the war bid

Crude oil fell 1.1% on the week to $91.40, per Morningstar, helped by a G7 coordinated fuel-reserve release that eased near-term inflation fears — but the underlying supply outlook stays hostage to the Iran conflict, and Thursday's ISM prices-paid spike showed energy costs still feeding through. Gold had a wilder ride: up more than 1% on Friday's soft dollar, but down 3.3% on the week to $4,144 as the earlier yield surge punished non-yielding assets, per Morningstar.

Earnings scorecard: this week's winners and losers

Winner: Accenture (ACN, +19.7% on the week). The week's single best large-cap earnings reaction — a 17–22% surge Thursday, its biggest one-day gain on record, per multiple outlets. Fiscal Q4: EPS $3.29 vs $3.18 expected, revenue $18.68B vs $18.03B expected (+6.2% YoY), new bookings $22.2B for a 1.2 book-to-bill, and a record 141 client deals worth $100M+. Full-year revenue hit $74.2B (+6%) with adjusted EPS of $13.97 (+8%). Sweetener: a $1B, five-year AI partnership with Anthropic. The tempering note, per TradingNews: fiscal-2027 EPS guidance midpoints slightly below consensus — the rally repriced collapsed expectations more than it confirmed acceleration. Next catalyst: October 14 investor day.

Loser: Nike (NKE, -3.6% Friday, -9.6% premarket). Revenue of $11.21B missed the $11.33B consensus (-4% YoY) though EPS of $0.48 beat $0.44. The killer was the first full-year fiscal-2027 guide: revenue down high-single-digits and adjusted EPS of $1.15–1.35 versus ~$1.65–1.69 expected. Greater China sales fell 22–26%, Nike Direct fell 8%, and the company announced job cuts under a "Pace" restructuring. The stock sits 54%+ off its 52-week high, per IBD.

The beat the market sold: McCormick (MKC, -7.7% on the week, new 52-week low). Revenue beat ($2.02B, +17.4% vs ~$1.98B expected) and adjusted EPS beat ($0.86 vs $0.76) — but GAAP EPS collapsed to $0.36 from $0.84 on special charges, organic growth was only 1.9% (the rest came from the McCormick de Mexico acquisition), and operating margin fell to 10.7% from 16.7%. A textbook quality-of-earnings selloff.

Honorable mention: Micron (MU). Adjusted EPS of $33.42 vs $31.72 expected on sales of $54.2B vs $51.3B (+379% YoY) — a monster print that the stock greeted with a ~1% decline. When a 10x earnings explosion can't lift the shares, expectations were already in the price.

Next week: the calm before the CPI

The coming week is light on tier-one data — no CPI, no PCE, no FOMC. The calendar: Monday brings ISM Services; Wednesday brings the minutes from the Fed's September meeting, the first detailed look at how officials debated the hike; Friday has Michigan consumer sentiment. Then the real tests arrive: September CPI on Wednesday, October 14 — the last major inflation reading before the October 28–29 FOMC vote — and PPI the day after. With the Shiller PE at 41.5, more than double its long-run mean, valuations leave little room for the bond market's version of events to be right.

The takeaway: the week ended with stocks up and bonds down — each market certain the other is wrong. October's data gets to decide. Until the 14th, the trade is simple: watch the long end, not the headlines.
