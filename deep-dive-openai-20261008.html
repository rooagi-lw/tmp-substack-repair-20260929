OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue is nearing $50 billion, not the $70 billion reported last month — and the $20 billion that "vanished" was never really there.

What happened

📉 The FT report — On Thursday, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI recently told investors its annualized revenue was "approaching $50 billion" at the end of September, citing financial documents shared with backers. That is about $20 billion less than the $70 billion figure reported by media outlets, including the FT itself, late last month.

🔍 Where the $70B came from — The earlier figure did not come from OpenAI. Per the FT, it was devised through "attempts by OpenAI's own investors to produce a direct comparison with Anthropic's annualised revenues." The two companies count differently: Anthropic includes gross revenue from sales via cloud partners such as AWS and Google Cloud, while OpenAI counts net revenue only. A source told CNBC the earlier $68 billion figure counted gross revenue including OpenAI's partnerships with companies like Nvidia.

📊 The market reaction — The Nasdaq fell 1.25%, its worst day since mid-August, while the S&P 500 dropped 0.5% (per CNN). The selling concentrated exactly where you'd expect: Nvidia fell 2.9%, Intel 5.3%, Oracle 5.5%, Broadcom nearly 4%, and Microsoft over 1%. The S&P 500's tech sector sank 1.8%, the worst of all eleven sectors (per Investopedia).

How we got here

OpenAI's revenue ascent has been genuinely historic — which is why the gap stung. The company went from about $1.6 billion in 2023 to $3.7 billion in 2024 to $13.1 billion in 2025, beating its own $10 billion internal target (per CNBC's February reporting on company disclosures). CFO Sarah Friar announced the annualized run rate had passed $20 billion in January; by February, Sacra estimated $25 billion. In June 2025 the company had announced a $10 billion run rate, excluding Microsoft licensing revenue.

Then came September 29: Axios reported the run rate was approaching $70 billion, up more than 70% since the start of Q3. That number — built on investor math, not company guidance — became the market's anchor. When the FT published the $50 billion net figure nine days later, the anchor broke.

By the numbers

The revenue is real and large. The spending is larger. Leaked 2025 financials (reported by Fortune) show $13.1 billion in revenue against $34 billion in total costs — a $20.9 billion operating loss. Cost of revenue was $7.5 billion, R&D $19.18 billion, sales and admin another $7.3 billion. The trend is improving — the company spent $2.37 for every dollar earned in 2024 versus $1.60 in 2025 — but the absolute burn is staggering.

Against that stands the buildout: Sam Altman cited roughly $1.4 trillion in infrastructure commitments over eight years in late 2025; by February, the company told investors it was targeting about $600 billion in compute spend through 2030 (per Reuters). CFO Friar reportedly questioned whether revenue growth could support those commitments and urged waiting until 2027 for an IPO — though she and Altman later called that reporting "ridiculous" and said they were aligned. An internal presentation cited by the FT projects cumulative negative free cash flow of $278 billion from 2026 through 2030.

The funding machine keeps pace: $122 billion raised in March at an $852 billion post-money valuation, and talks now for another $30 billion-plus at roughly $1.4 trillion (per Bloomberg). The confidential IPO paperwork filed in June has been pushed to early 2027, with Altman calling a 2026 listing "ill-advised" on AI safety grounds. Rival Anthropic, meanwhile, is expected to go public next month.

Why it matters

Annualized revenue is the AI industry's favorite yardstick — and its most abused. It extrapolates a single month's sales across a year, and as the FT episode shows, the number changes dramatically depending on who does the extrapolating and what they count. Yet the entire AI capex chain — Nvidia's chips, Oracle's cloud, Broadcom's accelerators — is priced on the growth story these numbers tell.

As Shay Boloor of Futurum Equities put it, the $20 billion gap is "simply the value of sales flowing through AWS, Google and Azure" — a reminder that "the winner of the AI model race keeps looking like whoever owns the distribution toll booth." And Baird's Ross Mayfield warned of "tremors throughout all of the related sub-industries" when the demand story wobbles.

Bull vs. bear

The bull case: Nothing was actually lost. The $20 billion was an investor-constructed comparison, not evaporated sales — OpenAI's net revenue run rate grew from $13 billion actual to ~$50 billion in about eighteen months, one of the fastest ramps in business history. Enterprise customers drive over 40% of revenue, unit economics are improving, and investors are still willing to fund the buildout at a $1.4 trillion valuation.

The bear case: $278 billion in cumulative negative free cash flow through 2030 against $600 billion-plus in compute commitments is a bet that requires everything to go right. Only about 5% of weekly users pay (per mid-2025 figures), ads are now being tested on free tiers, ChatGPT's share of AI web traffic fell from 87% to under 57% in fourteen months as Gemini surged, and the IPO keeps slipping. The supply chain is priced for the $70 billion world, not the $50 billion one.

What to watch next

  • The $30B raise — whether it closes at the $1.4 trillion valuation, and who writes the checks.
  • Anthropic's IPO — expected next month; public filings will finally let investors compare the two companies' real numbers.
  • Q4 run-rate updates — whether OpenAI's net figure keeps climbing toward the gross one.
  • Stargate spending decisions — build vs. rent, and who carries the debt.
  • Ad-tier monetization — whether free users convert or just tolerate ads.

Takeaway: The $20 billion was never lost — it was never OpenAI's by its own accounting. But the selloff revealed what the market actually fears: that the AI buildout is being priced on the bigger number, and nobody is quite sure which number is real.
